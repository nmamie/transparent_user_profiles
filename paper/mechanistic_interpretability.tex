\subsection{Mechanistic Interpretability}

To investigate how the fine-tuned recommender model internalizes textual user profiles and
responds to user interventions, we perform mechanistic interpretability probing using
\texttt{nnsight} (see Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix_interp} for full setup). Specifically, we probe
the final normalized layer of the transformer backbone, extracting mean-pooled hidden activation
vectors $\mathbf{h}_u \in \mathbb{R}^d$ across all user profile prompts. Probing this final layer
directly captures the model's final representation state before classification.

Our analysis proceeds in three stages. We first characterise the representational structure of
natural-language profiles and confirm that user edits move that representation
(\S\ref{sec:repr_structure}). We then ask whether this movement changes the model's predictions,
using both textual edits and direct activation steering (\S\ref{sec:repr_to_behaviour}). Finding
that it does not, we isolate the cause by asking whether the model personalises at all, and
comparing it against collaborative-filtering baselines on identical candidate sets
(\S\ref{sec:personalisation}). Throughout we report the controls that separate genuine effects of
fine-tuning from properties of the architecture, the pretrained backbone and the corpus.

\subsubsection{Representational structure and profile edits}
\label{sec:repr_structure}

As illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:perturbation}, natural-language user profiles form well-separated
groups in the model's 2D UMAP activation space. Figure~\ref{fig:perturbation} additionally tracks
the representation trajectory under counterfactual profile perturbations across three sequential
states (detailed in Table~\ref{tab:profile_perturbations}):

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{State 1 (Baseline Profile):} An unedited user profile expressing a preference for
          lighthearted romantic comedies.
    \item \textbf{State 2 (Weak Perturbation):} A modified profile shifting the user's interests
          towards the action domain.
    \item \textbf{State 3 (Strong Perturbation):} A heavily edited profile specifying a strong
          preference for dark crime, mystery thrillers, and detective investigations.
\end{enumerate}

When a user modifies their profile, we re-embed the perturbed text and trace the latent trajectory
vector ($\Delta d$). These edits produce smooth, continuous and directional shifts in the
activation space: textual interventions do measurably change the model's internal representation of
the user, rather than being absorbed without trace.

We caution against reading the cluster structure itself as a map of user taste. To test whether
these groups correspond to preference rather than to profile wording, we labelled $1500$ users by
the dominant genre of their \emph{actual} review history (romance $795$, crime $530$, action $175$;
a user is retained only when at least three of their reviewed items carry a genre and one genre
accounts for the majority of them) and compared those labels against $K$-means assignments over the
same representations. Agreement is at chance: adjusted Rand index $0.0008$ and normalised mutual
information $0.0038$, with every cluster reproducing the population base rate to within a few
percentage points (each contains $46$--$59\%$ romance users). A linear probe trained to recover the
same history-derived label reaches $0.449$ accuracy against a $0.530$ majority-class baseline; it
exceeds a shuffled-label control ($0.406$), and its macro-F1 of $0.356$ exceeds the $0.231$ a
constant predictor achieves, so the representation is not entirely uninformative about genre
preference, but the signal is too weak to beat majority-class accuracy. The clusters in
Figure~\ref{fig:perturbation} therefore organise profiles by lexical content, and the archetype
labels should be read as descriptions of profile language rather than as validated taste segments.

\subsubsection{From representation to behaviour}
\label{sec:repr_to_behaviour}

Movement in representation space does not entail a change in recommendations. We therefore test the
behaviourally meaningful question directly.

\paragraph{What drives a prediction.}
Table~\ref{tab:variance_decomposition} decomposes the variance of predicted ratings over a grid of
real profiles and catalog items. The natural-language profile is far from inert, accounting for
$47.6$--$48.5\%$ of the variance, comparable to item identity itself. It enters, however, almost
entirely as a \emph{user-level rating offset} -- an estimate of how generously a given user rates in
general -- rather than as a key matched against item content. Content-based personalisation must
live in the profile\,$\times$\,item interaction term, and that term accounts for only $4.1\%$ of the
variance when the model observes item titles alone, rising to $16.1\%$ once item descriptions are
also supplied. This bounds how much of the model's behaviour any content-conditioned steering could
possibly explain, and it motivates using the title-and-description configuration for every
content-matching analysis that follows: a title-only model never observes the genre evidence such an
effect would have to be computed from.

\paragraph{Textual edits.}
We assemble three genre pools (romance, action, crime) of $N{=}110$ catalog items each by weak
supervision over item descriptions, and score all $330$ candidates under each perturbation state.
Each state is expressed at the length of a real training profile, since the profiles used for
Figure~\ref{fig:perturbation} are $11$--$16$ words against a training median of $87$
(range $23$--$167$), and therefore fall below anything the model saw during fine-tuning.
Table~\ref{tab:behavioral_validation} reports the outcome. Editing the profile shifts predicted
ratings substantially and highly significantly ($+0.195$ at State~2, $+0.070$ at State~3, both
$p<10^{-9}$), but the shift is statistically indistinguishable across the three genre pools
(one-way ANOVA, $p{=}0.79$ and $p{=}0.60$). The residual differences point in the direction the
steering hypothesis predicts -- under the crime-targeting edit crime items receive the largest gain
($+0.086$ against $+0.059$ for romance) and are the only pool to move \emph{up} the ranking
($-3.9$ of $330$ positions, while action items fall by $3.9$) -- yet no contrast approaches
significance (crime against romance, one-sided $p{=}0.15$ for $\Delta\hat{r}$ and $p{=}0.31$ for
$\Delta$Rank), and the crime share of the top-$50$ recommendations moves only from $28\%$ to $30\%$.
A borderline result obtained at $N{=}40$ did not survive tripling the sample size.

\paragraph{Direct activation steering.}
A textual edit reaches behaviour through two steps, and the preceding result does not say which one
fails. We therefore intervene on the representation itself, adding $\alpha \mathbf{d}$ to the
residual stream at the profile token positions, where $\mathbf{d}$ is the difference of means
between the layer activations of crime-preferring and romance-preferring users. Before interpreting
any null we verify that the intervention propagates: a zero vector leaves the prediction exactly
unchanged, a \emph{constant} vector also leaves it unchanged because layer normalisation removes it
(so a steering direction must be non-uniform across dimensions), and a random direction does change
it. We further note that additive steering of the final representation cannot be selective at all:
the head is a single linear map, so $\mathbf{h} \mapsto \mathbf{h} + \alpha\mathbf{d}$ shifts every
item's prediction by the same $\alpha(\mathbf{w}\cdot\mathbf{d})$ and leaves the ranking untouched
(verified numerically to six decimal places). Steering must therefore be applied early enough to
interact with the item tokens through attention, which is what we do.

Table~\ref{tab:steering} reports the result, and it is a clean dissociation. The intervention moves
the latent state exactly as intended: the probe readout goes from $P(\text{crime}){=}0.064$ at
$\alpha{=}0$ to $1.000$ at $\alpha{\geq}4$, a complete reversal of the user's apparent genre
preference. The recommendations do not follow. Crime items' mean predicted rating \emph{falls}
slightly ($3.999 \rightarrow 3.984$) and their mean rank moves by at most $0.2$ of $330$ positions,
at every layer tested. We note that the probe reversal is in part guaranteed by construction, since
the injected direction is the same class-mean difference the probe reads; the behavioural null is
the finding. Driving the representation from one genre pole to the other, by the most direct means
available, changes essentially nothing about what the model recommends.

\subsubsection{Does the model personalise at all?}
\label{sec:personalisation}

The preceding nulls admit a simpler explanation than a failure of steering: the model may carry no
usable personalisation signal to steer. We test this with a task the reported metrics do not probe.
For each of $200$ users we take one held-out item they rated positively and $99$ catalog items they
never interacted with, and ask each ranker to place the held-out item within that set. A percentile
of $0.5$ is chance. Crucially, all rankers see identical candidate sets.

Table~\ref{tab:personalisation} shows that the task is very much feasible on this data: BPR reaches
$0.867$ and WMF $0.806$. The profile model reaches $0.542$ in its title-only configuration
($p{=}0.047$) and $0.510$ with descriptions ($p{=}0.62$, not significant). It is therefore
marginally above chance at best, and is outperformed by \texttt{MostPop} ($0.697$), which performs
no personalisation whatsoever. Consistent with this, the profile model's scores are uncorrelated
with item popularity (Spearman $-0.03$ to $-0.08$, both $n.s.$) even though the held-out positives
are substantially more popular than the distractors ($81.6$ against $41.9$ training interactions),
so it fails to exploit even the popularity signal available in the candidate sets.

The ordering in Table~\ref{tab:personalisation} identifies the cause. Models trained with a ranking
objective (BPR, WMF) reach $0.81$--$0.87$; models trained to regress observed ratings (MF, and the
profile model in both configurations) reach $0.51$--$0.55$. MF and BPR consume exactly the same
interaction data and differ only in their loss, so that gap isolates the objective rather than the
information available. The profile model lands precisely where the other rating-regression model
does. This is the expected consequence of the training signal: every training pair is an item the
user actually chose and reviewed, so the data contains no mismatched user--item pairs from which a
content-matching rule could be learned, and the loss is minimised instead by predicting a user's
general rating level together with an item-specific offset -- exactly the structure
Table~\ref{tab:variance_decomposition} exhibits.

This also explains why the finding is compatible with the ranking metrics reported in
\S\ref{sec:results}. Those use test-set reranking over the Sakai condensed list, which orders only
items the user already selected. Ordering a pre-filtered set of chosen items is achievable from
item-side priors alone and does not require distinguishing relevant from irrelevant candidates. The
limitation we identify is therefore a property of the rating-regression objective inherited from the
original formulation, not of natural-language profiles as an interface: on the same data, a ranking
objective attains $0.87$.

\subsubsection{Controls for the attribution analysis}
\label{sec:attribution_controls}

Figures~\ref{fig:attribution} and~\ref{fig:global_attribution} report token-level gradient
attribution over profile tokens. Because such a pattern could equally reflect the architecture, the
pretrained backbone or the profile corpus, we repeat the global analysis under three controls -- the
pretrained GPT-2 backbone with a randomly initialised regression head, the same architecture with no
pretrained weights, and the fine-tuned model applied to word-shuffled profiles -- and compare all of
them against corpus-frequency and subword-count baselines. Since gradient magnitudes are not
comparable across differently scaled models, we summarise each condition by a scale-invariant
statistic: the ratio of mean attribution on a fixed a-priori genre lexicon to mean attribution on
all remaining profile words.

Table~\ref{tab:attribution_controls} reports the outcome, and it does not support reading these
figures as evidence that fine-tuning concentrates attribution on preference features. In the
fine-tuned model, genre words receive roughly \emph{half} the attribution of other profile words
(ratio $0.546$, $p{=}0.002$), and no genre term appears among the top $20$; that list is led by
\texttt{widescreen} together with discourse and style words (\texttt{however}, \texttt{while},
\texttt{including}, \texttt{levity}, \texttt{cinematography}, \texttt{soundtracks}). Eleven of those
top $20$ words recur in a \emph{randomly initialised} model, and attribution correlates at
$\rho{=}0.807$ with the fine-tuned model applied to word-shuffled profiles, indicating that the
pattern is driven by which words are present rather than by their arrangement or meaning. Attribution
is moreover negatively correlated with corpus frequency ($\rho{=}-0.320$, $p{=}0.027$), i.e.\ it
tracks word rarity; the number of subword tokens per word is not a significant driver
($\rho{=}0.212$, $p{=}0.148$). We therefore treat these figures as descriptive of the saliency
method's behaviour and do not claim that they isolate features acquired through fine-tuning.

\subsubsection{Summary}

Taken together, the picture is coherent. Profile edits do move the model's internal representation,
and that movement is smooth and directional (\S\ref{sec:repr_structure}). But the representation
organises profiles lexically rather than by validated taste, the genre axis it contains is weak, and
driving that axis from one pole to the other -- whether through text or through direct activation
steering -- leaves recommendations essentially unchanged (\S\ref{sec:repr_to_behaviour}). The reason
is that the model carries little usable personalisation signal to begin with: it is outperformed by
item popularity on a held-out ranking task that a ranking-objective baseline solves at $0.87$
(\S\ref{sec:personalisation}). Scrutability, in this formulation, gives a user control over how
generously they are modelled rather than over what they are recommended. We read this as a
constraint of the rating-regression objective rather than of editable natural-language profiles, and
a concrete direction for future work: training the same interface against a ranking objective, with
negative sampling, is the change most likely to make profile edits behaviourally meaningful.
